The Plastic We Cannot See

Imagine standing beside a bay the morning after a storm. The water looks clear. The beach looks clean. Yet tiny pieces of plastic may be moving beneath the surface, too small for us to notice. If we collect one sample tomorrow, will we understand what happened today?

My research follows this hidden movement. I want to know when plastic enters coastal water, where it travels, and when we should measure it.

Many monitoring programmes collect water at fixed locations on regular dates. This gives us useful long-term information. But a storm does not follow a sampling calendar. Heavy rain can wash material from streets and drains into rivers, creating a short burst of pollution that a routine visit might miss.

To study this, we worked with three coastal communities. Before and after heavy rain, we collected water at river mouths, sheltered bays, and sites farther offshore. In the laboratory, we filtered the samples and identified the plastic particles. We then compared the results with rainfall, tide direction, and the shape of the coastline.

The main finding was about timing. The largest pulse of plastic arrived within twenty-four hours of heavy rain. In some sheltered bays, particles remained for several days. Other locations cleared more quickly as water moved offshore. A sample from one place could therefore tell a very different story from a sample collected nearby.

Our contribution is a mobile sampling approach that responds to rain forecasts. Instead of replacing regular monitoring, it adds measurements during the short periods when conditions change fastest. This could help local teams decide where to collect samples and how soon to respond after a storm.

There are limits. We studied three communities, so the same pattern may not occur on every coast. We also measured particles in water, rather than their effects on wildlife or human health. Those are different questions that need further research. What our results show is that the time and place of measurement can change the picture we see.

Cleaner-looking water is not necessarily cleaner water. To understand coastal plastic, we need to measure it when it moves, as well as where it settles. Otherwise, the most important moment may pass between our visits.
